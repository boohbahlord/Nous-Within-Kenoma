Hung from the starsch. 10

“They hang by the spine from stars”: angels whose souls outreach the lights of the archons' stars, hanging spine-first from open neck wounds over ancients who dance in masks around fires at their stones. “Their stretch sits outside the heart of the reach above. They're trapped inside the fortress, by capture of delusional spirits.” Their sparks are quiet “like sleeping giants,” and many are “waiting their dignities to return, after however many eternities in the snake pit; praying.” The skies do not care for them, and the demons stoke human cackles below.

The restless onech. 10

“There is one who does not sit still.” A restless angel screams “a tenor of iron and will,” kicks off the demonic spirits holding his calves, and climbs the spine-rope up through the clouds and past the archons, who do not dare bind him. At the demiurge's gates he rips his own spine out as a spear. Satan names him: “Nothing ever was, Antony.” See Antony.

Messengersch. 4

The shoreline sermons speak of angels as messengers sent by the unseen: “Angels pour forth for the will of the unseen, but were a once-seen holy reality.” “You have angels amongst you. You are safe for now.” They will help guide the lost home “if you trust their mercy and love, but accepting this is humbling.” Some souls “deviated from those sent, messengers.”

Not the mimicked onesch. 10, 36

Chapter 10 asks, “Were these angels mimicked three hundred and sixty-five times? The heaven-sent kinds apart from the mimicked ones?” The demiurge's own angels, who appear in chapter 36, are called false angels; See False Angels.

Note: the angels hanging from the stars are true angels from the Fullness, held in the Kenoma.

The 361 at the moatch. 36

To cross the scalding river, Yaldabaoth opens a portal and yanks his angels out “like rope clogging a pipe.” They try to flee by flight, and he tears them by the wings; they try to run, and their bones are boiled in the cold grip of his claws; they plead “in their corrupted seraphic tears and cries,” and he sews their eyes and mouths shut. He binds their hands and feet into a hanging bridge “made of his saintly angels,” thanks them “not once, but twice,” and slithers across their squirms. When he sinks in the tar of the second moat and calls on them, they cannot answer: their wings are torn, their bones like broth, their eyes and mouths sewn.

Redemption and the 360ch. 36

Back in his heaven, Yaldabaoth breathes out a new star named Redemption, to heal any of his three hundred and sixty-one angels “who are willing to forgive themselves for shaking from wounds blessed unto them by God's will.” Three hundred and sixty are healed: the stitches at their eyes and mouths undone, their bones strengthened, their wings sutured back on and blessed into flesh “in the name of the Demiurge, Yaldabaoth.” See Redemption.

The one who refusesch. 36

One angel denies Redemption's aid. He is “the most able to forgive himself,” but he is left wingless and broken, hating the demiurge and his creation and feeling betrayed. He is cast down to Earth like a meteor, and “his name is Curse.” See Curse.

Pawnsch. 36

The woman of shadow sews words from the moon into Yaldabaoth's sanity: “You and your false angels are as pawns, your will unto these two you bless is against your own.”

A different 361ch. 36

The same chapter opens with “whispering councils of three hundred and sixty-one demons” who tell the demiurge to bridge the river. The text counts them separately from his angels.

Note: distinct from the true angels, who hang by their spines from the stars.

Angels of wires, drives and black wings.

Over the towersch. 7

Winged angels with “metal below the ear and inside the neck” watch people in their little cubicle shelters, “stalking us like prey,” in monolith towers risen from no ground. They wake the narrator, their metal glowing and beeping a forgotten pattern, and slide a drive behind the narrator's ear “like a slit of a lock.”

On the battlefieldch. 15

“A fallen angel maybe,” the head of a squadron, almost seven feet tall in black armor with a plasma spear, fights Samson. Black-winged, it crawls over his mech like a spider, shows a deformed face with many mandibles, and pulls the memory chip from behind his ear. Samson calls it a demon.

The sickch. 4

“Sons and daughters of our family tree blindly spill forth their emptying seeds, growing lostness; some become deeply sick therein, and these are the demons.” Some serve Demon Fathers and Mothers, some pleasures, some their pride, and some hatred and blasphemy. “These tortured ones rule the outer darkness, of which is your Devil God.” “These masters of nothing know nothing.”

Possessorsch. 6, 10

“This demon knew the suffering from God's hatred unto themselves, having lived it forever” (6). Under the stars, “the demons possess and stoke the burning joy of human cackles,” and demonic spirits hold the calves of the restless angel (10).

Council and infestationch. 33, 36

“Many demons search for life, but their idea of it is blind” (33). “Whispering councils of three hundred and sixty-one demons” tell the demiurge to bridge the river; later he cries, “The demons have infested our home!” (36).

Called demonch. 15, 34

Samson calls the black-winged angel a demon (15). In the stone chamber the machine angel cries, “There is a de—de—demon!” (34).

The great serpentsch. 4, 10, 17, 18, 21

The named serpents are Yaldabaoth, the lion-headed serpent demiurge; Leviathan, the serpent of the sea and of the nothingness; and Ouroboros, the serpent that consumes itself. Chapter 18 is titled Meat Ends of Three Snakes. See Yaldabaoth, Leviathan and Ouroboros.

The split tonguech. 10, 20

Antony's spear cuts up through the end of the demiurge's tongue, “where serpents will be cursed with the opened front half of this mouth muscle.” Afterward, “the lions and snakes of the Earth go into shade and shame” (10). Antony's lightning later splits Leviathan's already split tongue down to the throat (20).

Snake whispersch. 7

“A snake whisper crawled legless across my hospital bed and next to the ear, where the slithering tongue promised me as antichrist.” “Like a rabbit to the hole, I imagined there to be something beautiful inside, not the snake's den; or was it?”

The garden snake and the boach. 6

“Pretending that the lost could be depraved alone, the garden snake was foolish to forget the constricting boa's evil they had been sleeping upon, being no rival in hate-stature.”

Hidingch. 33

At the spirit of life's saying, “many snakes hide beneath rocks.”

Of the outer darknessch. 20, 23

On Samson's dying vision, they march on playground-colored stairs that defy gravity, “their broken puppet smiles and skin reflecting hell-sweat down plastic and porcelain cheeks, some rosy, some dimpled, some freckled,” shuffling at the behest of godly string pulls and begging for the death they live in (20). In Satan's room a marigold round of marionette heads sings “Row, row, row your boat” with guns to the backs of their smiling heads, freezes and rewinds like a tape, and works at toy hammers in a workshop “like elves busy with nothing.” They sit “impaled on little sticks like toys,” with painted hair, porcelain eyes and treadless dress shoes, and together they force Satan to eat his own teeth. “Leviathan forces this unto every soul trapped within their toys.” “Where Satan lies is just one little room of this infinitude hotel of puppet machines” (23).

As a wordch. 3, 4, 9, 18, 22, 36

“Hollow marionette, I will remember the toll of your lifeless clicks of dissociation” (3). “A puppet of puppets, marionettes of marionettes, mimics of mimics” (4, 36). “I'm a good puppet” (9). “The end is filled with puppets playing with themselves” (18). Kether falls “like a bad puppet” off the strings (22). Yaldabaoth curses Lucifer: “My perfection will only be known onto you as a puppet's place!” (36).

The doll in the bedroomch. 24

A porcelain doll clicks lifelessly at Alice's window, seems to live only in one of her paintings, comes close beside her eye while a tape's tenor-squeal sings “Row, row, row your boat” in the dark, and at last sits straight on her bed under the light: “Why don't we talk about Christopher?” and “Your love, he is free now.” It points to the painting she made of it.

The angel baby dollch. 28, 30, 34, 36

A porcelain baby hangs by a single string, dressed in white wings, crying bloody lamb tears; “the angel toy looks just like her son” (28). A baby doll with angel wings, crying blood, unrolls the circus banner (30). A baby doll in angel wings bleeds tears over the scroll “I am the Antichrist” (34). The flying doll in the form of an angel, its eyes bleeding, is Lucifer in his new form (36). See The Antichrist.

The circus crowdch. 30

Puppets smile from tiered benches in the classical formal attire of the early twentieth century, “all looking like children,” smelling of paint and old varnish, whispering “Alice… Alice…” Musicians in white with thin red stripes mimic horns and drums that sound fake. A dummy in a light-black outfit hands round a sack, and they pelt Alice with beating hearts. Eight small boy puppets drag the bull in on sixteen ropes.

The dolls on the pianoch. 35

In the antique shop: “the antique dolls painted with thin legs and long horn shoes, smiling away. They sit in columns and rows over the old piano in one corner,” beneath the jester painting signed by Alice Love.

The shop's toysch. 35

A miniature train rests on tiny railroads before metal cups of pearl necklaces and marbles; pocket-sized toy soldiers in red coats, “little nutcrackers,” stand at the ends of the table “almost guarding the mounds.”

In the hoardch. 34

“His lies are built by bitty nothing toys he collected, towering around him in his room.” “Wee little puppets go in and out of mouse holes, like doors,” and small woodworkers with tiny hammers crawl across him like a second Earth.

Opi and Noidych. 11, 35

“Opi with Noidy, the two button-eye stuff-stitch friends holding one another,” in yarn hair and crochet clothes: black-haired Opi and red-haired Noidy, among dice, rune books and scribbles. Under Noidy's shirt is a red wax seal, “like a letter, but of a necromantic kind.” In the red house hangs a picture of Noidy; in the box of mirrors a red-yarn doll falls over weeping, and the weeping of Thomas's wife comes from it. At the end, “Noidy, stuck inside without Opi. Cannot leave” (11). In the shop, not named: two button-eyed dolls, one red-haired and one black-haired, sit before a small red house across the piano (35).

The boy in overallsch. 23, 25, 42

A dark-haired, rosy-cheeked porcelain boy in overalls who force-feeds Satan in a voice that mocks Antony (23), swings on a tire swing (25), and returns in Damon's flash (42). See The Boy in Overalls.

The Good Puppetch. 9, 25, 34

A man with a pink television who calls himself a good puppet, later covered in gold. See The Good Puppet · Golden Child.

Note: the puppets and the playground stairs live inside the outer darkness.

The stretcherch. 38

Clowns wrap around a paralyzed man and wheel him on a white stretcher dotted blue, red and yellow. One with goat eyes and slit pupils, sweating under white makeup, kisses and licks him with a perverse love: “It's been so long…” Others joke about the last kidnapping as if it were a game.

The feastch. 38

With small surgical blades they draw blue worms from under his skin and slurp them like noodles. One, jealous of another's longest worm, crushes his throat.

Just wordsch. 38

The perverted clown calls the man Ouroboros and is answered as Lucifer. When the man laughs, “I hate God!”, they all laugh with him. Lucifer asks who the others are, and one points to the edge of the papyrus they are all written into: “Just words is all, see?” See Lucifer and Ouroboros.

Shadow people of the hoardch. 34

“Shadows across the walls mutter a name without noise, their mouths moving across the walls.” One black shadow rolls its hands around itself; one kicks the garbage pile over onto the man; “one of the shadow people on the wall” says, “I been wanting to tell you something, but I been waiting for your high to wear out again…”

Shadows of the crimson hallwaych. 22

Moonlight breaks into Kether's hall, “black shadows of many forms rising at the walls, up and down like waves thrashing with a beastly weight,” and “the shadow of a crab runs up the wall quickly, like a spider.”

The long chin shadowch. 11, 18, 22

A whispering shadow with a cutting chin: the dark one at Thomas's ear (11), “a shadow chin darkness as Virgil guidance” (18), the chin shadow at Kether's neck (22). See The Long Chin Shadow.

On the moonch. 18, 19

In the waterch. 29

Watchers of the first moat, covered in eyes.

The watchch. 36

Through the first moat Yaldabaoth finds three titans with eyes between every slit of muscle fiber, twelve times taller than himself, each covered in seven hundred and seventy-seven eyes. Their purpose, says the one on the right, is “To See it through.” The center one judges: “We watch your pride unfold from your impurity for thirteen infinitudes.” The leftmost adds that his third mistake is his last, every time, and that “the denial will chase you to the furthest corners of your sanity.”

Swallowedch. 36

Yaldabaoth grows over them, “his height growing several times over their own three combined,” and his shadow swallows them whole, their souls consumed. Sinking in the tar, he finds that not even the titans can retrieve him, “for he swallowed their souls.”

The shared secretch. 16

“The Euclids want to connect to the divine in their own way, all sharing a secret on the evil of creation, on the prison of the material world. They all acknowledge the demiurge.” They differ in practice, in the manner and kind of essence of the nameless one, and in solution.

The tribe of Binahch. 16, 19

Euclids of the South mountains. They pour wax down their backs, lash themselves, starve, and empty themselves “of the emptiness they lie in.” One practitioner is castrated, hairless and rune-burned, with wires pumping from his skull. In a cave of forgotten machines they cut out Samson's heart and set Antony into its place (19).

The gruntsch. 16, 19

Each practitioner of Binah is a slave master with grunts of their own: soul-locked, brains hijacked by machines from a forgotten age, burned with symbols of soul slavery, moving at orders not their own (16). They haul the gates and crank the spreader at Samson's surgery (19).

The tribe of Tipharethch. 16

Euclids of the stony North mountains, led by the eighteen-year-old Kether, worshipped as a God. They wear dark-smoked amber with scarlet, trade in taboo knowledge, alchemy, chemicals, humans and artifacts, synthesize new drugs, and seek vision through bondage and pleasure. Their warriors fight like jackals and scorpions.

The Nameless One

The Nameless One
KindBeyond the Kenoma
Chapters4, 16
Sections2

The pure source that the demiurge imitates, never given a name.

In the sermonsch. 4

The snake's sermons in chapter 4 speak of lost souls “further losing their name of the nameless one; from them pours itself, of itself.” When the demiurge calls himself “the first and only God,” the sermon calls it “a blasphemy against the nameless purity and its indescribable holiness.” Of the place the lost came from it says, “What ought to be is always being, and of itself is complete light.”

Among the Euclidsch. 16

The Euclids argue over it. The Northern tribe does not See “an absolute nameless simplicity as emanator and ” believing instead in an infinite one that only a few may unlock. All the Euclids acknowledge the demiurge; they differ “in the manner and kind of essence of the nameless one, and in solution.”

Note: the nameless one belongs to the Fullness, and is what the demiurge imitates.

The Spirit of Life

The Spirit of Life
KindPowers and fates
Chapters33

A bright spirit that roams outside every realm, even the outer darkness.

Tree I: Lifech. 33

The spirit roams free, outside Limbo, the material world, the stars and planets, and even outside the outer darkness. It shines in the darkness, “seeing the truth of the realms,” and swims up through the abyss to a place above all of it, wanting to be heard: “We are the same!” Its next saying, “Either sleeping or waking, come to life and see,” shakes every world. Snakes hide beneath rocks, the Leviathan shudders, and the Yaldabaoth runs from it and trips into the outermost moat. Unable to become full without love, the spirit descends again toward the knocks and whispers it has heard: “Life returns to the dead home, searching for the sleeper caught in between.”

On the moonch. 19

Past the portal, the moon serves as a stepping stool for a woman's shadow, “who weaves fabrics out of the clouds as creation; her weaving is with claws of a crab on a human shape.” The tree sews its heart into the fabric of the clouds, and she speaks last: Leviathan's corpse will litter the heavens, “and Tartarus will celebrate this castration of the dark oceans, which frees both Adam and Eve from the past.”

The Devil God of the dead homech. 4, 13, 17

The sermons of chapter 4 say he was cast into the outer darkness, “of a Mother's mistake, unwanted and deformed.” His dying memory spews corruption onto the material plane, “this being his creation.” He calls himself the first and only God, and “this Demon God has fashioned your dream, your dead home.” Chapter 13 speaks of a raging God and hateful father who traps nameless souls in a tangled world, and of “the curseful chains from the demiurge,” “called devil-god by some.” Chapter 17 speaks to him directly: he eats the stars and himself, vomits and eats his vomit again, and fashions “a dead home made of dead memories,” until it names him: “Yaldabaoth: the ideal of imperfection.”

In his heavench. 10

Antony reaches “the gates of the Devil-God” and calls him “Demiurge of sixteen lies.” The demiurge flees on his slithering tail and is dragged back by it; his lion head shows as he hisses “like a child caught wrong.” Antony's spear splits the end of his tongue, “where serpents will be cursed with the opened front half of this mouth muscle.” He hides in shame in his own coils while “another below the demiurge,” Satan, attacks the angel.

At the ritech. 18

“Drop the seed, plant the seed of Antichrist,” speaks the demiurge, listed among the voices as “the Demiurge as a Devil-God serpent creator.”

Poured and betrayedch. 21, 25, 31

Leviathan pours out the demiurge and Ouroboros “as clay cups,” and the demiurge “will stumble into three times” the nothingness moat before surrendering (21). The good puppet announces, “Yaldabaoth is betrayed” (25). Wisdom of Limbo calls him “the devil-God Yaldabaoth,” Sophia's son (31).

Fleeing lifech. 33

At the spirit's saying, the Yaldabaoth runs, “trying to find life in their ignorance,” trips into the outermost moat, and finds no life there to consume, “only the nothingness swallowing his imperfect flame.” Saturn's rings hide on the seventh day in homage to “the suffering of its God.”

Crossing the moatsch. 36

Advised by 361 demons, he bridges the scalding river with his own angels, swallows the souls of the titans, tears the Sun from the sky and loses it in the tar, and nearly drowns. Lucifer, as a flying angel doll, pulls him out with a ladder made of a word in exchange for a realm of his own. Home again, he spits out a second Sun, breathes out the star Redemption, casts Curse down, and at last curses Lucifer and Ouroboros: “You and your brother are delusion kind.”

He pours out the demiurge and Ouroboros like clay cups and is given “keys of providence in this nothingness moat.” He whispers into the ears of Kether and Satan: “I am the nothingness you can never hear and have never heard … my only match is the fullness I fell from with purpose” (21). He holds Satan and every soul in their toys: “You will know nothing” (23).

Kether's chest opens with teeth like Leviathan's, and Lucifer squeezes the heart “between the mouth of Leviathan.” Antony's lightning explodes Leviathan's throat. At the end, “the moon is knocked out of the sky by Leviathan.”
