No. 54

The King's Festival

he King of the courtyard has the men rally, with horns screeching a harmony that tells of an oncoming festival. Folk of every class are all around, singing drunkenly, dancing a jolly jig, using up the seats and benches every which way, and their slurred words carry the stress of work off into the gusting air. The colors are plentiful, and they collect into an amusement that is childishly simple. Ladies in their spring-plucked flowers mingle about in public like never before, while their husbands are lost to care in empty bottles. The birds and bunnies stray and scatter across the warm and sunny day to collect every kind of abundance left behind. Even the trees seem to sway to the song with the mountains as the sun watches a joust.

When sunset falls, the wine is sucked down thrice as fast, and barrels of beer are drained through straws in less than an hour. The carnival show peaks under the night, but the clouds cover the stars with a blanket of canvas black. The moon does not show its face yet. The Jester swings from the circus tent, playing a swell of volume like the swaying suspension of fish hooks, and his talent brings out an amplified, careless attitude in the peasants, priests, scribes, and knights sitting next to one another at the show. The instrument is nothing but a dizzying kind of thoughtlessness, and the crowd sits back and laughs insensitively at the little children pretending to play along below, who dance in a sober frown of strange fear at the rashness of their parents, a fear as bitter as warm alcohol. One boy pouts with a puckered lower lip and a crunched chin, his tears and babble lapped up into elation by parents who are far gone into glut, like fish drowning in drink.

tranger and stranger the next act becomes. Clowns come out blowing kisses in a group, as a man with eyes filled to the brim with cries and screams is wheeled in: the prisoner who hated God through an unspeakable act of mockery. The tallest clown, adorned in a thick white ruffle skirt, hangs a key above the prisoner’s gullet and drops it down to be swallowed. Another clown wheels in a tray of knives and scalpels. They run in their large, round, empty but flaring pants covered in dots of red, blue, and yellow, which catch the wind like sails full of air. The bulge of the prisoner’s neck shoots blue in thin and thick wiring under his skin, then turns red in his screams. The spectators roll over in a death of laughter for a reason the little kids cannot understand, which makes more of them cry, but their fathers and mothers do not care for them in the otherworldly state coming on.

Poisonous ale is poured over his face, but he does not want the drink, and his face shutters away from it in a yank anyway. There is the sadistic grin, like the look of a fat bully, as the largest clown holds the prisoner’s mouth open with his dry white gloves of powdered taste, yanking at the teeth like the pry of a crocodile’s mouth. The prisoner yelps through an open mouth that is quickly becoming parched, his eyes rolling every which way at the top of the tent, before his eyelids flutter fast shut as a forceful surge of steam drenches him in a burn of ten proof, coating his bare neck and face. The cheering begins to roar. The young ones stomp and beg for the show to end, but to no avail.

One small and very freckled kid, a little lanky, with a straight, black bowl of hair, begins offering a sack to the audience, going between the rows at the columns of stairs, where many reach over one another to pull out the snacks that wiggle in the bag of their own accord. There are loud buzzing beetles that some chomp down with a maul, while others prefer the thick but stubby caterpillars, blue, curled fetal, hissing, with a taste of bad shrimp. One especially tiny girl tries to slap the blue stubby bug out of her mother’s hand, but she is slapped hard in the face, and then anywhere else. The woman is now raging recklessly, her palm stinging red across the little one’s head and body. The daughter cannot be heard over the rising noise all around the tent.

By now, the clowns have begun their surgery, cutting the prisoner up and letting out his blood, as a practice to spill the evil. The man straightens out into a plank with a slobbering mouth, his expression wide open, as if alive, yet without seeming dead. The clowns all take turns cutting silly patterns of slices, like the patterns of their costumes or the marquee of the canopy.

None are left in boredom, though, for another yank of a carriage carries in a podium, which other clowns set up with impressive swiftness. The other prisoner is now dragged in by a chain around his feet, his hands bound in cuffs. They hurry him in, naked, trembling, skinny with the fat of famine, pulled by friction through the dirt beneath the stage. The first prisoner, his cold sight locked in a bloody mess, is pulled away, and the head of the new one is shoved up through the single hole in the stage. The new clown, who rode in as the passenger of the carriage, performs magic tricks that make birds disappear like a death unseen. He has a hidy-ho attitude and long but sturdy arms, neck, and legs. He runs to the back of the theatre drapes, which are covered in rain clouds, and eventually returns with a ventriloquist dummy whose mouth drops between its slits, dressed in formal attire like a royal court’s fashion. The dummy sits on the lap of the buffoon, on a stool next to the prisoner’s head poking out. Claps rise as the dummy waves its hand through the air, its uncanny look scaring the toddlers especially.

More bottles of velvet wine are passed about by the freckled tall boy, and all of the debauchery is fueled as some puke away their earlier indulgence. Some peasants slap and hit one another in chaos, out of control from the alcohol’s confusion. The show begins when the dummy crowns its fake hair with fake gold. The replica of a boy looks up and down, pulls out a scroll, and reads, “I am the one who wrote this, in the writer’s own words, and when it is read by anyone other than myself, the moon will bleed on my fateful night. I take pleasure in destroying myself, for hate destroys its temple from God. When I, delusion, am dead, clarity will rain from the clouds.” The observers stand up from their seats and throw tomatoes, booing in hatred and screaming for his damnation. The prisoner who wrote this was only a man, named.

lsewhere, the King is sober. He is with another, rolling dice over cards, seeing what may come. The dice land on three numbers that total eighteen. The one card they land on shows a man in a blindfold, wrapped in rope. The water in the bowl ripples with a red like obliviation. A messenger knocks at the door. The King almost ignores it, then, after a pause, tells him, “Yes, come in. What is it?”

Doors of tall wood open onto a hall, and the man, slender yet fit, is sweaty and panting as he says, “Lord! The prisoners are executed!”

Rolled and clenched in the man’s knuckles is a manuscript, and the King nods at the servant before asking, “What about the scroll in your hand?”

Quickly, the man answers, “This was written by the prisoner you hated most.”

He rubs his fingers at the end of his grey and white beard and demands with calm interest, “Bring it here, then.” The King gestures the writing towards himself before requesting that the Sir leave. Now at his table, he carefully rolls it open at the bunched and crinkled part. There is writing in a thick ink.

Aching Fate. King, you have become my whore and slut. Waiting for the dice before glass. It is on this desk that the moon foams red as me. The death blacks poison write. Sin invited itself into your Kingdom. I, Lucifer, wear your circus like a glove, letting blood pleasure. Unfolding itself, where my scroll becomes the sky, moon, and ground. My intruders wear joy. The show watches these sayings as my burden. Possessed by my demon called drink, and their temples hold me here. Are you listening? Your world is snatched up at my brother’s tongue; won’t you inquisite the world back out the mouth? Waiting to take hold.

Watch yourself swallow these words. The spectacle follows.

Faintly sweaty, his brow raised in astonishment, the King wears an immediate look of disbelief. The card and dice almost make him ready to flip the table now. He stands up tall and rushes to the tinted glasswork, and where the sun is placed within it, the moon in the night sky peeks through the curtains of the clouds, bleeding. There is a hissing whisper as he watches the clouds roll off the planet: “Who is in control now?”

Gritting his old teeth, he pops his eyelids open wide. He stomps towards the table and throws his arm across it, spilling everything off its edge. An angered cold, sweating in a time that sits still, wears him, and his mind is lost for prayer. The miracle of death does not find him in his fear of God, where he feels confined in a seeping burn that erodes his sanity like a wet torch in the head. The King’s tongue ties up in a stutter like a maraca; the tip of it is gripped like the throat of a snake’s revenger. The temperature and the heart build up to the peak of the ride before a word inside his head rings loud: “Make no doubt about it, you bleed the words that fall out of your mouth into a writing over my skin, and it makes me sick. Your neck will bend like a crook if you call out the name of a Lord you do not know.”

Like a darting rabbit, the King’s eyes snap into every corner, prey searching for a hole to crawl out through, but his senses melt with a rumbling gut screaming chaos. His appetite is filled with the sayings. He feels he has no choice. He cannot make it back if he fights it, so he gives his word to keep his own tail; something comes alive in his skin when he slides its cover away.

Unannounced, the door opens, and in a maddening drench of stress crawling out of his skin, his pupils become dots that dart in instant reaction. A knight covered in blood stands at the ready for his behest: “Lord! The court has scattered! The peasants throw themselves at one another in the violence beneath a fallen tent, others go after the shops to steal, and even more chant the death of your Royalty! What must we do, King?!”

Chattering, his jaws slap and maul the inside of his mouth, and in a gnawing reaction of detestation and loathing, he screams the command, “Demons are afoot! Kill every one who is possessed under the blood of the Moon! Their fault is their own! Slaughter the hateful without mercy!”

In a firm, loud reply, the knight nods his head: “Yes, my Lord! Your will is done!”

The horses trample skulls into fractures, and the gallops gush the guts of all those caught in the net of the tent, under its rose-and-milk pattern. Peasants bite at the soldiers like dogs with disease, while some who passed out drunk have a spear dropped with force through their chests. Children run screaming into their homes with tortured weeps that stifle in terror at the impending doom. One peasant sets fire to the Church before a Knight runs him through with a weapon in retort. Red, red, and more red, until the whole Kingdom is drowned in it.

Yet the constant execution continues into the morning, until no parents are left and the offspring have become orphans. The King is found dead with his scroll shoved down his gullet and his eyes plucked out and chewed in his gaping mouth. The scroll is removed, but only a scribble of black ink is found all across it, resembling no language, over the words it covered. The children now curse the moon in their prayers to their fostering God. Yaldabaoth shepherds the little ones with sympathy, becoming a loving father for once, sad at the destruction of his town, which he did not will, and mourning the King he appointed over it.
